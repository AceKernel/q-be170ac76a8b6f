# Purchasing power parity and real exchange rates

With $S^{A/B}$ in units of $A$ per $B$ and comparable basket prices $P_A,P_B$:

$$S_{\mathrm{PPP}}^{A/B}=\frac{P_A}{P_B},\qquad
\frac{S_1}{S_0}=\frac{1+\pi_A}{1+\pi_B},\qquad
\frac{S_1-S_0}{S_0}=\frac{\pi_A-\pi_B}{1+\pi_B}.$$

Use the exact relation unless approximation is explicitly requested (Tutorial 3 instruction). The approximation is $\Delta s\approx\pi_A-\pi_B$. For several periods, compound annual factors; for cumulative inflation, do not compound the cumulative rate again.

$$E_t^{A/B}=\frac{S_t^{\mathrm{actual}}}{S_t^{\mathrm{PPP}}}
=S_t^{\mathrm{actual}}\frac{P_{B,t}}{P_{A,t}},\qquad
1+\Delta E=(1+\Delta s)\frac{1+\pi_B}{1+\pi_A}.$$

With actual comparable price levels, $E=1$ means absolute PPP. $E>1$ means denominator currency $B$ is overvalued in this convention. With arbitrary CPI base indices, focus on **changes**: $E_1/E_0$; the numerical level need not equal one under PPP. Relative PPP keeps the initial real deviation constant, not necessarily zero.

For a Big Mac quote in LCU/USD, local-currency overvaluation is $S_{\mathrm{PPP}}/S_{\mathrm{actual}}-1$. Do not confuse it with USD overvaluation $S_{\mathrm{actual}}/S_{\mathrm{PPP}}-1$.

High-inflation currencies tend to depreciate nominally under relative PPP. Deviations do not automatically imply tradable arbitrage: transport costs, taxes, non-tradables and barriers matter. A real depreciation can improve export/import-competing competitiveness; operating effects depend on prices, quantities and sourcing.
